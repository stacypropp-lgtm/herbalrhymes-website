Dry Skin, a Dry Cough, and a Heavy Heart: Why Autumn Brings All Three (and the One Organ Behind Them)

In Chinese medicine the Lung governs your skin, your breath, and your capacity to grieve. Here’s what the classical texts say — and a 14-day plan that treats all three at once.

By Dr. Stacy Peck, DACM, L.Ac. October 28, 2026 9 min read

By Dr. Stacy Peck, DACM, L.Ac.

Every year around the end of October, the same three complaints walk into my life within a week of each other: skin that suddenly flakes and tightens, a tickly dry cough that won’t turn into anything, and a low, heavy sadness that has no obvious cause. Western medicine files them in three different departments — dermatology, respiratory, mental health. Chinese medicine files all three under one organ system, and it has for roughly two thousand years.

That organ is the Lung. This post explains why the oldest medical text in the Chinese tradition links your skin, your breath, and your grief to the same place — and what to actually do about it, with every claim checked against two source texts: the classical Huangdi Neijing Suwen and Giovanni Maciocia’s The Foundations of Chinese Medicine, the standard modern textbook. Points are verified against Deadman’s A Manual of Acupuncture.

One organ, three complaints

Chinese medicine organizes the body into five organ systems, each paired with a season, a climate, a tissue, and an emotion. Autumn belongs to the Lung. The Suwen’s fifth chapter lays out the full set of Lung correspondences in a single passage: the direction west, the climate dryness, the taste acrid, the body tissue skin and body hair, an emotion that translators render as sorrow, worry, or grief, a sound (weeping), and a characteristic disturbance: coughing.1 Read that list again: skin, grief, weeping, cough. The three complaints in my first paragraph are all sitting in one sentence of a text written around the Han dynasty. Maciocia summarizes the same system in modern terms: the Lung “controls skin and body hair,” is injured by dryness, and is the organ most affected by sadness and grief.2

So when the air dries out and the light drops, the organ system that governs your skin, your breathing, and your capacity to let go is the one under the most seasonal stress. Three symptoms, one root. That is not a metaphor in Chinese medicine; it is the diagnostic logic.

Your skin is the Lung’s outer layer

In the Suwen, the Lung “generates skin and body hair.”1 Maciocia explains the mechanism: the Lung disperses defensive qi and body fluids to the space between skin and muscles, which is what keeps skin moist, warm, and resilient. When Lung qi or Lung fluids are weak, the skin is the first tissue to show it — it becomes dry, rough, and loses its glow.2

This is why, in a Chinese medicine clinic, dry autumn skin is not treated as a surface problem. A richer moisturizer helps the way a tarp helps a leaky roof. The underlying issue is that the fluids being delivered to the skin from inside have dropped, and the autumn air is pulling more moisture out. Both sides of that equation can be addressed.

Check yourself: If your skin is dry and your lips crack and your throat is scratchy on waking, you are looking at dryness affecting the Lung system as a whole, not three unrelated issues. In the Foundations, dry skin, dry throat, and a dry cough with little or no phlegm are listed together under Lung dryness and Lung-Yin deficiency.2

The dry cough that isn’t a cold

A fall cough that never produces anything, worsens in heated rooms, and comes with a tickle in the throat is the Lung reacting to dryness. Maciocia lists “dry cough, dry throat, dry mouth, dry skin” as the signature of external dryness invading the Lung, and the same cluster — plus a hoarse voice and night sweats — when the Lung’s own Yin is depleted.2 The Suwen is blunter: when dryness dominates, things dry out.1

The practical distinction that matters: this cough does not need expectorants (there is nothing to expel) and it rarely needs suppression. It needs moisture delivered to the Lung — through what you drink, what you eat, and the air you breathe at night.

Grief lives in the Lung

The Suwen describes what grief does to the body in one line in its thirty-ninth chapter: when a person grieves, “the qi dissipates.”3 Maciocia unpacks the clinical picture: sadness and grief deplete Lung qi, producing shortness of breath, tiredness, a weak voice, and a tendency to cry — and because the Lung governs the skin, long-standing grief can also leave the complexion pale and the skin dry.2

This is a two-way street, and that is the useful part. A weak Lung makes a person more susceptible to sadness; sadness further weakens the Lung. The autumn drop in light and warmth nudges the Lung into deficiency, which is why so many people feel inexplicably heavy in late October and November even when nothing in their life has changed. The Suwen’s advice for the three months of autumn is to keep the mind “peaceful and tranquil,” collect the spirit, and “cause the Lung qi to be clear.”4 In practice, the fastest way to do that is through the breath.

If the heaviness is more than seasonal — if it lasts most of the day for more than two weeks, or you have lost interest in things you normally enjoy — please talk to a healthcare professional. Chinese medicine is a complement here, not a replacement.

Skincare from the inside: a 14-day protocol

Everything below targets the same root — moistening and strengthening the Lung — so the dry skin, the dry cough, and the low mood are addressed together rather than one at a time. Give it two weeks; fluids rebuild slowly.

1. Moisten from the inside

  • Warm water, all day. Not iced. Room-temperature or warm water is absorbed with less effort by a system that is already working hard. Aim to never feel thirsty.
  • Add moistening foods. Classical Chinese dietetics uses foods that are themselves listed as Lung-moistening medicinals. Honey (Feng Mi) “moistens the Lungs and stops cough”; black sesame seed (Hei Zhi Ma) nourishes Yin and moistens dryness; lily bulb (Bai He) moistens the Lung and calms the spirit.5 Pears, white fungus (tremella), almonds, and pear or Asian-pear soup are the traditional kitchen versions. Add one of these to every day.
  • Reduce what dries you. Alcohol, coffee beyond one cup, very spicy food, and sugar all pull fluids. The Suwen pairs the Lung with the acrid taste and then warns, in the same passage, that heat injures the skin and body hair and that excess acrid does the same.1 In autumn, go easy on heat and spice.

2. Humidify the room you sleep in

You spend a third of the season breathing bedroom air. Furnace air in a northern climate is often below 30% relative humidity, which dries the nose, throat, and skin all night. A humidifier near the bed, cleaned weekly, is the single most effective change most people can make for a dry autumn cough.

3. Press these four points daily

Two minutes each, firm pressure, both sides, once in the morning and once before bed. Locations and indications are from Deadman’s A Manual of Acupuncture.6

  1. Lung 9 (Tai Yuan) — on the wrist crease at the base of the thumb, in the hollow just on the thumb side of the pulse. The Lung’s source point; tonifies Lung qi and is indicated for cough and dry throat.
  2. Kidney 6 (Zhao Hai) — in the hollow directly below the tip of the inner ankle bone. Nourishes Yin and benefits the throat; indicated for dry throat and the kind of night-time dryness that wakes you.
  3. Large Intestine 11 (Qu Chi) — at the outer end of the elbow crease when the arm is bent. The Large Intestine is the Lung’s paired organ; this point is a classic for skin conditions, itching, and dryness.
  4. Ren 17 (Shan Zhong) — the center of the breastbone, level with the nipples. The meeting point of qi; indicated for chest oppression, sighing, and shortness of breath — the physical signature of grief. Press gently and take five slow breaths.

4. Breathe for the Lung — and for the grief

Once a day, sit upright, exhale fully, and let the inhale happen on its own. Six slow breaths, extending the exhale slightly longer than the inhale. In the Suwen’s terms this is how you “collect the spirit qi” and clear the Lung in autumn.4 Pair it with the one thing in your life you have not let go of. Autumn is the season for exactly that.

When your body is trying to tell you something

Dry skin, a tickly cough, a wave of sadness — separately, these are easy to dismiss. Together, in autumn, they are a message from one organ system asking for moisture, warmth, and rest. That is the way Chinese medicine reads every symptom: not as a malfunction to silence, but as information about where the body is out of balance.

I wrote Your Body Is Trying to Tell You Something to teach that reading skill for 50+ everyday symptoms — dry skin, cracked lips, cold hands, 3am waking, afternoon fatigue, and more — so you can tell what your body is asking for before it has to shout.
