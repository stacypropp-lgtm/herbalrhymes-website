Why Fall Makes You Sad: What Chinese Medicine Says About Grief and the Lungs

Feeling weepy or heavy every autumn? In Chinese medicine, fall is Metal season — and its emotion is grief.

By Dr. Stacy Peck, DACM, L.Ac. October 17, 2026 7 min read

In This Article

  1. Why am I sad every fall? The Chinese medicine answer
  2. Grief and the lungs: a two-way street
  3. What to do when fall grief shows up
  4. When it's more than seasonal
  5. The season isn't the enemy

Every October, it happens. The light thins out, the air gets sharp, and out of nowhere you're crying at a dog food commercial — or just carrying a low, gray heaviness you can't quite name.

It's not just you. And it's not just the shorter days.

In Chinese medicine, autumn has an emotional weather system all its own — and it runs through your Lungs.

Why am I sad every fall? The Chinese medicine answer

Chinese medicine maps the year onto the Five Elements, and autumn belongs to Metal. Each element is linked to organ systems, a color, a flavor — and an emotion. Metal's organs are the Lung and Large Intestine. Its color is white. Its flavor is pungent. And its emotion is grief.

This isn't metaphor for decoration. In this framework, emotions and organs shape each other. Grief is the emotion most closely tied to the Lungs — and the Lungs are most vulnerable in the season they rule.

Look at what autumn actually is: everything letting go. Leaves drop. Gardens die back. The year exhales. The whole season is one long demonstration of release — and if you're holding anything unreleased, fall will find it. Old losses surface. You feel weepy "for no reason." There's a particular ache that arrives with the first cold snap, like your body remembers every ending at once.

That's not dysfunction. In Chinese medicine, that's resonance — your inner state vibrating with the season. The question is never "how do I stop feeling this." It's "how do I let it move through."

Grief and the lungs: a two-way street

Here's the part most people miss: the relationship between grief and the Lungs runs in both directions.

In Chinese medicine, each emotion is associated with an organ system, and when an emotion gets stuck — suppressed, unexpressed, looping for months — it can injure its organ. Stuck grief weighs on the Lungs. Anyone who's sobbed knows the physical truth of this: grief lives in the chest. It constricts the breath. Held-in sorrow literally tightens the ribcage over time.

But it goes the other way too. When Lung energy is depleted — by a dry autumn, by shallow breathing, by the season itself drawing energy inward — grief becomes harder to move. You can't quite cry it out, and you can't quite shake it. It just sits there, like fog.

So fall creates the perfect loop: the season stirs grief, grief taxes the Lungs, taxed Lungs can't clear the grief. Understanding the loop is the first step to breaking it — because now you know it's not a character flaw. It's physiology, as Chinese medicine sees it.

There's another layer worth knowing. In this tradition, the Lungs govern the breath — the most literal rhythm of taking in and letting go your body performs. Every inhale receives; every exhale releases. When grief gets stuck, that rhythm gets stuck with it: shallow breathing, sighing, a chest that never quite opens. Working with the breath isn't just calming down. It's speaking the Lung's native language.

What to do when fall grief shows up

You don't need to fix the season. You need to give grief somewhere to go. Here's what I'd suggest — all of it try-tonight simple.

Breathe it out — literally

The exhale is the letting-go half of the breath, and most of us barely do it. Try this: inhale through your nose for a count of four, then exhale slowly through your mouth for a count of six or eight. Make the exhale longer than the inhale. Do ten rounds.

In Chinese medicine terms, the long exhale helps Lung energy descend and disperse instead of bunching up in the chest. In plain terms, it tells your nervous system the emergency is over. Do it when the heaviness shows up — in the car, in the shower, at 3 a.m.

Press LU1 — the grief point

There's an acupressure point called Lung 1 (Zhongfu) that practitioners associate with grief held in the chest. It's easy to find: locate the hollow just below your collarbone, near your shoulder. Drop about a thumb's width below that hollow and slightly outward toward your arm. Roll your fingertip around until you find a tender spot — that's it.

Press firmly (not painfully) for two to three minutes on each side, breathing slowly while you do. People often feel the chest soften, or an unexpected lump in the throat — that's the point doing its job. Suppressed emotion needs an exit, and this is one of the doors.

Eat for the season

Metal's flavor is pungent — and pungent foods help Lung energy move. Think ginger, garlic, onion, scallion, radish, daikon, a little horseradish. A small amount in warm, cooked food goes a long way. (One caution from the tradition: too much pungent is drying, and autumn is already dry. Season, don't scorch.)

Metal's color is white, and white foods are considered especially supportive this season: pears, apples, daikon, cauliflower, rice, oats, almonds. The classic is a poached pear — simmer a peeled, sliced pear with a coin of fresh ginger for fifteen minutes. It's sweet, warming, and in Chinese medicine it's the go-to for dry autumn lungs. Make it tonight; thank me tomorrow.

Guard your neck

This one sounds like your grandmother, but there's real theory behind it: in Chinese medicine, wind is considered a carrier of seasonal illness, and it enters at the exposed neck and upper back. A light scarf on windy days — even before it feels like winter — protects the boundary the Lungs depend on. Your wei qi, the defensive energy distributed through the Lung system, lives at the surface. Keep the surface warm.

Let yourself cry

Here's your permission slip: crying is not a breakdown. In Chinese medicine, tears are grief in motion — the emotion leaving the body instead of lodging in it. A good cry that moves through and lifts is the system working correctly. What injures the Lungs isn't grief itself; it's grief with nowhere to go.

The letting-go list

Autumn is the season of release, so give it something to work with. Tonight, write down three things you're holding that the season is asking you to release. An old argument. A version of yourself that doesn't fit anymore. The perfect summer that ended. You don't have to solve any of them — just name them. In Five Element terms, the Large Intestine (Metal's partner organ) governs elimination: letting go of what the body no longer needs. The emotional version counts.

When it's more than seasonal

A brief, honest note: seasonal heaviness that lifts — that moves through with tears, breath, rest, and time — is one thing. Sadness that doesn't lift, that deepens week after week, that comes with hopelessness or the inability to function, is another. That deserves real professional support, not just blog advice. There's no weakness in that; it's just accurate triage. If you're unsure which one you're in, talk to someone you trust — or a professional — sooner rather than later.

The season isn't the enemy

Fall grief isn't a malfunction. It's your system resonating with a season built around release — leaves, light, the whole bright summer exhaling. The sadness is information, not failure. And once you know the loop — grief taxes the Lungs, tired Lungs trap the grief — you can work it from either side: move the breath, press the point, eat for the season, let the tears come.

The trees don't apologize for dropping their leaves. You don't have to apologize for feeling the season change.
