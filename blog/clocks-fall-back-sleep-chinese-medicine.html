Clocks Fall Back on November 1: Why You’ll Sleep Worse This Week (and the 2,000-Year-Old Fix)

The Neijing has a specific sleep rule for autumn and winter — and the time change pushes you toward it. Four acupressure points and a 5-day plan for the transition.

By Dr. Stacy Peck, DACM, L.Ac.

At 2:00 a.m. on Sunday, November 1, 2026, clocks across most of the United States fall back one hour. Everyone talks about the “extra hour of sleep.” Almost nobody talks about the week that follows: wide awake at 4:30 a.m., foggy by mid-afternoon, and strangely unable to fall asleep at night even though it is pitch dark by 5 p.m.

Chinese medicine has a specific, two-thousand-year-old instruction for exactly this time of year — and, oddly, the clock change pushes you toward it, not away from it. This post covers what the Huangdi Neijing says about autumn and winter sleep, why your body fights it, and four acupressure points for the transition that are different from the ones in my bedtime routine post. Every claim is checked against the classical text and a modern standard: Deadman’s A Manual of Acupuncture for points and Maciocia’s Foundations of Chinese Medicine for theory.

What actually happens on November 1

Your clock moves, but the sun does not. In Chicago, sunset on November 1 lands around 4:45 p.m. standard time, and it keeps getting earlier through mid-December. Sunrise, meanwhile, jumps an hour earlier overnight. Your body’s internal clock is set by light, not by what your phone says, so for about a week it is running on the old schedule: you get sleepy an hour “early” and wake an hour “early.”

Most people fight this. They stay up to their usual bedtime by the new clock, push through the early waking, and spend a week sleep-deprived. The Neijing suggests the opposite.

The 2,000-year-old sleep rule for autumn and winter

The second chapter of the Suwen gives a sleep instruction for each season. For the three months of autumn: “Go to rest early and rise early, get up together with the chicken.” For the three months of winter: “Go to rest early and rise late. You must wait for the sun to shine.”1 The same chapter says the sages “in autumn and winter nourish the yin” — and warns that going against the autumn pattern harms the Lung, while going against the winter pattern harms the Kidney.1

Two things stand out. First, the instruction in both seasons is bed early. Second, as the season deepens, the morning instruction shifts from “rise with the rooster” to “wait for the sun.” Late October to early November is the hinge between those two, and the clock change lands right on it.

Maciocia frames the same idea in terms of Yin and Yang: autumn and winter are the Yin half of the year, when the body’s Yang qi draws inward and the correct response is more rest and earlier nights. Sleep itself is the daily version of the same cycle — Yang qi entering the Yin at night — so the seasonal and daily rhythms either reinforce each other or fight.2

The practical translation: after November 1, let your bedtime move earlier by the clock instead of holding it in place. If you were in bed at 11 p.m. before the change, your body now thinks 10 p.m. is 11. Go with it. That is the Suwen’s instruction, and it is also what your light-set body clock is already trying to do.

Why you fight it (and what it costs)

The reason the early-bed rule is hard is simple: electric light and screens hold the Yang qi up and out when the season is asking it to sink. In the Neijing’s model of sleep, defensive qi circulates in the Yang (exterior) during the day and enters the Yin (interior) at night; when it cannot enter the Yin, “the eyes do not close.”3 Bright evening light, late eating, and a mind still processing the day all keep that qi on the outside.

The cost is more than tiredness. Maciocia lists the sleep-related consequences of Yin not being nourished in the cold months: difficulty falling asleep, waking in the night, a dry mouth and throat at night, and the kind of restless, “wired but tired” feeling that is so common in November.2 It is also, not coincidentally, when a lot of people start reaching for a second glass of wine to get to sleep — which makes the night-waking worse.

Four points for the transition week

These are different from the five points in my bedtime routine post, which target falling asleep and the 3 a.m. wake-up. This set is chosen specifically for the seasonal shift: two points that regulate the Yin–Yang switch of sleep itself, one for the racing mind, and one for the heart. Locations and indications are from Deadman’s A Manual of Acupuncture.4 Press each firmly for 1–2 minutes, both sides, within the hour before bed.

  1. Kidney 6 (Zhao Hai) — in the hollow directly below the tip of the inner ankle bone. Nourishes Yin, calms the spirit, and is indicated for insomnia; it also benefits a dry throat, which is why it earns its place in heated-air season.
  2. Bladder 62 (Shen Mai) — in the hollow directly below the tip of the outer ankle bone. Indicated for both insomnia and excessive daytime sleepiness — in other words, for a sleep–wake switch that is stuck. In the classical model these two ankle points open the Yin and Yang Qiao vessels, which govern the closing and opening of the eyes.3 Press them together: thumb on Kidney 6, index finger on Bladder 62, squeeze gently. Modern clinical trials of this pairing for insomnia report improved sleep quality on polysomnography.5
  3. Pericardium 6 (Nei Guan) — three finger-widths above the inner wrist crease, between the two tendons. Calms the mind and regulates the Heart; the point for the brain that will not stop running through tomorrow.
  4. Heart 7 (Shen Men) — on the inner wrist crease, on the little-finger side, in the hollow just inside the tendon. The Heart’s source point and one of the most frequently used points for insomnia in the clinical literature; indicated for insomnia, poor memory, and agitation.

Skip the ankle pair if you are pregnant and check with your provider first; otherwise all four are safe to self-press.

A 5-day plan for the clock change

  1. Thursday–Saturday (Oct 29–31): Move bedtime 15–20 minutes earlier each night. Dim the lights after dinner. Press the four points before bed.
  2. Sunday (Nov 1): Get outside within an hour of waking, even if it is overcast. Morning light is what resets your internal clock to the new time. The Suwen’s winter rule — wait for the sun — is literally about this.1 Eat dinner early and keep the evening dark.
  3. Monday–Wednesday: Hold the earlier bedtime. If you wake at 4:30 a.m., do not reach for your phone — press Kidney 6 and Bladder 62 together, breathe slowly, and let yourself drift. Most people are fully adjusted by mid-week.
  4. All week: A humidifier in the bedroom. Dry heated air and a dry throat are a leading cause of the 3 a.m. wake-up in November, and Kidney 6 can only do so much against a furnace.
  5. Through the winter: Keep the earlier bedtime. The Suwen does not say “adjust for a week”; it says rest early for the whole Yin half of the year.1
