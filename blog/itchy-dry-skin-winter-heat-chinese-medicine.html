Itchy, Dry Skin the Minute the Heat Comes On? Chinese Medicine Explains (and What to Do)

Winter itch is a Blood pattern, not a skin problem. The classical texts, the three-point acupressure routine, and an inside-and-outside plan that works in 4–6 weeks.

By Dr. Stacy Peck, DACM, L.Ac. October 28, 2026 7 min read

By Dr. Stacy Peck, DACM, L.Ac.

There is a specific week every year — usually late October in the Midwest — when the furnace kicks on for the first time and within days the shins start itching. Then the forearms. Then the back, right where you can’t reach. The skin looks fine, or maybe a little flaky, but it itches, especially at night and especially after a hot shower.

Dermatology calls this winter itch, or xerosis. Chinese medicine has a more useful name for it, because the name tells you what to do. This post explains the pattern, what the classical texts actually say about itching, and a plan that works from the inside and the outside at the same time. Claims are checked against the Huangdi Neijing Suwen and Maciocia’s Foundations of Chinese Medicine; points are verified in Deadman’s A Manual of Acupuncture.

Why heated air makes you itch

Two things happen when the heat comes on. Outside, the cold air holds very little moisture. Inside, you warm that air without adding any, so the relative humidity in a heated home often drops below 30% — drier than most deserts. Dry air pulls water out of the outermost layer of skin, the barrier cracks microscopically, and nerve endings that normally sit quietly under a moist surface start firing. Hot showers strip what oil is left. Wool and fleece finish the job.

That explains the trigger. It does not explain why some people itch every winter and others never do, or why the itch is worse at night, or why it often comes with dry eyes, brittle nails, and feeling colder than everyone else in the room. For that, Chinese medicine has a pattern.

The Chinese medicine pattern: Blood deficiency generating Wind

In Chinese medicine, Blood does more than carry oxygen. It is the moistening, nourishing substance that keeps tissues supple — skin, hair, nails, tendons, eyes. The Suwen puts it concretely: the Liver receives Blood and so can see; the feet receive Blood and so can walk; the hands receive Blood and so can grasp.1 When Blood is insufficient, the tissues it should be moistening dry out, and in the skin that produces dryness, flaking, and — the key symptom — itching with nothing much to see.

Maciocia lists the signs of Blood deficiency: dull-pale complexion, dry skin, dry hair, dizziness, poor memory, insomnia, numbness or tingling, blurred vision, scanty periods, and a pale tongue.2 When the dryness becomes severe enough, the pattern tips into what the tradition calls “Blood deficiency generating Wind”: Wind in Chinese medicine is anything that moves, shifts, and comes and goes — and an itch that migrates from shin to forearm to back, worse at night, is textbook internal Wind arising from dryness.2

The Suwen has a line on itching that practitioners have debated for two thousand years. Among its nineteen “pathomechanisms” in chapter 74: “All pain, itching, and sores belong to the Heart.”3 The Heart governs the Blood. Whatever the commentators argue about that line, the clinical reading is consistent: persistent itching is treated by addressing the Blood, not by sedating the skin.

Does this sound like you? Itchy, dry skin that is worse at night and after showers; dry eyes or blurry vision when tired; brittle or ridged nails; hair that sheds more in winter; feeling cold easily; trouble falling asleep. Three or more of those together is a Blood-deficiency picture, and the itch is the loudest symptom, not the only one.

Two other patterns can cause winter itch and are worth ruling out. Lung dryness gives dry, tight skin with a dry cough and dry throat but little itching (see my post on why dry skin, a dry cough, and a low mood arrive together in autumn). Damp-Heat gives red, weepy, hot, intensely itchy patches — that is eczema territory and needs a practitioner, not a blog post.

The inside plan: nourish Blood

In Chinese medicine, Blood is made from food by the Spleen and stored by the Liver. The Suwen says that when a person lies down, Blood returns to the Liver1 — which is one reason sleep and Blood go together and why Blood-deficient people often sleep badly. So the inside plan has three parts: feed Blood, protect digestion, and sleep.

Blood-nourishing foods

  • Black sesame (Hei Zhi Ma) — listed in the Materia Medica as nourishing Blood and Yin and moistening dryness. A tablespoon of black sesame paste or ground seeds daily is the single most-prescribed food in my clinic for winter skin.4
  • Red dates (Da Zao) and longan (Long Yan Rou) — both nourish Blood and calm the spirit; simmer a few into morning oatmeal or congee.4
  • Goji berries (Gou Qi Zi) — nourish Liver and Kidney and are traditionally used for dry, blurry eyes.4
  • Dark leafy greens, beets, bone broth, eggs, and red meat if you eat it — the kitchen-level versions of the same principle. Cook them; raw, cold food in winter taxes the Spleen that makes the Blood.

Protect what makes the Blood

Warm, cooked, regular meals. Skipping breakfast, eating cold salads in November, and running on coffee until 2 p.m. are the three fastest ways to weaken the Spleen and starve the Blood. This matters more for winter itch than any cream.

Sleep before midnight

Blood returns to the Liver when you lie down. The Suwen’s rule for the cold months is bed early, rise with the sun.5 Nourishing Blood with late nights is like filling a bath with the drain open.

Three acupressure points for itchy skin

Press each firmly for two minutes, both sides, once or twice a day. Locations and indications from Deadman’s A Manual of Acupuncture.6

  1. Spleen 10 (Xue Hai, “Sea of Blood”) — with the knee bent, two thumb-widths above the top inner corner of the kneecap, on the bulge of the inner thigh muscle. The classic point for itching, urticaria, and eczema; it invigorates and cools the Blood.
  2. Large Intestine 11 (Qu Chi) — at the outer end of the elbow crease when the arm is bent. Indicated for itching of the skin, urticaria, and dry skin; the Large Intestine channel runs along the forearm where winter itch so often starts.
  3. Spleen 6 (San Yin Jiao) — four finger-widths above the inner ankle bone, just behind the shin bone. Nourishes Blood and Yin and calms the spirit; it also helps the sleep that Blood-building depends on. Skip this one during pregnancy.

The outside plan: stop the leak

  • Humidify the bedroom to 40–50%. Clean the unit weekly. This is non-negotiable if you itch at night.
  • Shorter, cooler showers. Hot water strips the skin’s oil. Keep it warm, not hot, and under ten minutes.
  • Moisturize on damp skin within three minutes of getting out, with a plain, fragrance-free cream or oil. Sesame or almond oil works and fits the Chinese medicine logic of moistening.
  • Cotton or silk against the skin; wool and fleece over it, not on it.
  • No scratching with nails. Press or tap instead. Scratching breaks the barrier and invites the Damp-Heat pattern you do not want.

Give the inside plan four to six weeks. Blood is built slowly, and the skin you are moisturizing today was made a month ago.

Your skin is a messenger

The itch is not the problem. The itch is your body telling you that the moistening, nourishing substance that keeps every tissue supple has run a little low, and the first place it shows is the surface. Treat the surface and the message gets louder next year. Treat the Blood and the skin follows.
